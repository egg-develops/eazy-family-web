In many Swiss families, grandparents are far more than occasional visitors. They collect grandchildren from Kita on a fixed weekday, host the Wednesday-afternoon lunch, drive to swimming practice when both parents work late, or fly in from Italy or France for school holidays. The rhythm of the week often depends on them just as much as it does on the parents.

Yet most family organization tools quietly leave grandparents out. The WhatsApp family group has become a mix of memes and forwarded messages. The parents' shared calendar sits inside one couple's Google account. School emails go to two addresses. Grandparents end up asking the same questions again and again — not because they are disorganized, but because nobody has given them a real seat at the table.

A **family app for grandparents** — really, a family organizer that treats grandparents as full members — solves this quietly and without drama.

## Why grandparents get left out of digital family tools

Most family apps were designed with a specific unit in mind: two parents and their children under one roof. When a family tries to add a grandparent, one of three things usually happens.

The grandparent is added as an extra "adult" but sees every private note between the parents. Or they are not added at all and receive forwarded screenshots instead. Or they install the app, cannot find what they need, and quietly stop opening it. The person who most needs a clear view of the week — the one on Tuesday pickup — ends up with the least reliable information.

## What a good family app for grandparents looks like

A tool that works for the extended family has a few characteristics that are easy to miss in a feature list.

**Clear, calm interface.** Grandparents do not need gamification or a busy dashboard. A shared week view with clear names, colors, and times is enough.

**Granular access.** Grandparents should see what is relevant to them — the shared calendar, tasks that involve them, messages about pickups — without being pulled into private conversations between the parents.

**Notifications that respect their day.** A ping for every shopping-list item is noise. Notifications should focus on what involves the grandparent personally: an upcoming pickup, a message addressed to them, a change to a plan they are part of.

**Multilingual by default.** In Switzerland, it is normal for a family to speak German at home, French with grandparents in Vaud, and Italian with grandparents in Ticino or Lombardy. Each member should be able to use the app in their own language while sharing the same events, tasks, and messages.

**Web-first access.** Many grandparents are comfortable with a browser but not with installing another app. Opening the family organizer in a browser tab, the same way they open email, makes participation frictionless.

## Where grandparents fit in the shared calendar

The biggest win from including grandparents is not a new feature. It is silence. The endless "are you still picking up on Thursday?" messages stop, because the answer is already visible to everyone.

A few patterns work well in Swiss households:

- **Recurring pickups and drop-offs** — Wednesday afternoon at grandma's, a Friday-morning ride to school — belong in the shared calendar as recurring events, not ad-hoc messages.
- **School holidays and camps** are easier to plan when the grandparent sees the cantonal school-holiday dates in the same tool. See our guide to [school holidays in Switzerland and how a family app helps](/resources/blog/school-holidays-switzerland-app).
- **Doctor's appointments and clubs** should include a note about what the child needs to bring, so whoever is on pickup duty has the same information.
- **Birthdays and gatherings** get one home in the family calendar rather than being scattered across chat threads and email invitations.

## Grandparents at a distance

Not every Swiss family has grandparents nearby. Many international families have grandparents in Portugal, the UK, India, or the United States. A shared calendar lets them see the school play on Thursday, the dentist appointment next week, and the ski week in February. A private family chat gives them a place for the small things — a photo from the concert, a note about a card in the mail — without competing with work emails.

## Cozi, FamilyWall, and the extended-family gap

Established organizers like Cozi and FamilyWall are strong on shared calendars and lists, but were built primarily for the North-American nuclear family and monetize through advertising. Adding grandparents to an ad-supported product means feeding another set of habits, ages, and routines into an advertising profile — often across borders. For Swiss and European families, a private, ad-free organizer is a better cultural fit.
